@round-turn-two-half-hitches|Round Turn & Two Half Hitches|round-turn-two-half-hitches-knot|Hitch|1|Everyday,Camping,Boating|Round turn and two half hitches
=Attach a line to a post with friction turns and a simple finish.
+Soft practice rope and a smooth post or ring.
!Keep hands clear of loaded turns. Actual mooring or rigging needs suitable rope, an adequate object and the complete prescribed securing method.
?The rope makes the round turn around the object, followed by two same-direction half hitches around the standing part.
xReversing the second half hitch changes the finishing pattern.
>Go around the object::Take the working end around the post and return it toward the standing part.
>Add the round turn::Continue around once more, leaving two visible turns around the object to add friction.
>Make the first half hitch::Wrap the tail around the standing part and tuck it through its own loop. Snug it near the post.
>Repeat the same direction::Make another half hitch around the standing part, turning the same way as before.
>Dress the finish::Slide the two half hitches together, keep the turns orderly and leave a generous tail for inspection.

@running-bowline|Running Bowline|running-bowline-knot|Sliding loop|2|Boating,Camping|
=A sliding eye formed by a small bowline around the standing part.
+Practice rope and an inanimate object; learn the Bowline first.
!The outer loop tightens around its contents. Never put it around a person, limb or animal, and do not use this practice version for lifting.
?The small bowline is fixed, but the standing rope slides through it to change the large eye.
xTying an ordinary bowline without enclosing the standing part gives only a fixed eye.
>Return around the object::Take a long working end around the practice object and bring it back across the standing part.
>Plan the small eye::Arrange a small bowline eye that encircles the standing rope, leaving the larger loop around the object.
>Begin the bowline::Make the small crossing loop, bring the tail up through it and around its standing section.
>Complete the collar::Feed the tail back down through the small crossing loop, leaving it inside the bowline's eye.
>Inspect the two loops::Dress the bowline, then gently pull the main standing rope to observe the larger loop sliding closed. Keep hands outside that loop.

@san-diego-jam|San Diego Jam Knot|san-diego-jam-knot|Fishing attachment|2|Fishing|Reverse clinch
=A coiled hook-eye connection with an upper-loop finishing tuck.
+Practice fishing line and a blunt ring.
!Wrap count depends on line diameter. Keep coils adjacent and test the finished connection rather than relying on the knot's name.
?The tag passes through the small eye-side opening, then back through the upper loop.
xTightening before the upper-loop tuck is complete can leave an unfinished connection.
>Thread and hold a loop::Pass the tag through the eye. Fold it back alongside the standing line and pinch a loop above the eye.
>Wrap the doubled section::Wind the tag around both the standing line and its returning section several times, progressing toward the eye.
>Use the eye-side opening::Pass the tag between the two line sections immediately beside the eye.
>Return to the upper loop::Bring the tag back up and pass it through the loop held at the top of the wraps.
>Set the coils::Moisten and ease the knot toward the eye. Keep the wraps from climbing over one another; inspect and test before trimming.

@sheepshank|Sheepshank|sheepshank-knot|Rope shortening|2|Everyday,Camping|
=A traditional way to shorten a rope temporarily without using its ends.
+Long soft practice rope, laid out on a table.
!Unreliable when tension changes or the rope is slippery. Study the traditional form only; do not use it to bypass damaged rope or carry a critical load.
?Two end bights are held by half-hitch loops on the standing parts, with a straight middle section between them.
xAssuming it stays secure when slack ignores the chief weakness of this knot.
>Lay an S shape::Fold the rope back on itself twice to make three parallel sections and a bight at each end.
>Make the first half-hitch loop::Twist a small loop in the standing part beside one bight.
>Capture the bight::Place that small loop over the adjacent bight and draw it gently around the folded section.
>Repeat at the other end::Make the matching half-hitch loop on the other standing part and place it over the other bight.
>Observe its limitation::Apply light, steady hand tension to see the shortening effect. Release the tension and notice how easily the arrangement can fall apart.

@sheet-bend|Sheet Bend|sheet-bend-knot|Bend|1|Everyday,Camping,Boating|Weaver's knot
=Join two ropes, using the thicker or stiffer one as a folded bight.
+Two contrasting practice ropes, preferably with slightly different diameters.
!Can work loose with slippery material or intermittent load. Do not use as a critical rope join without appropriate instructions.
?The thin tail and thick tail emerge on the same side; the thin tail is tucked under its own crossing.
xOpposite-side tails create the less secure left-handed form.
>Fold the thicker rope::Make a U-shaped bight in the thicker or stiffer rope and hold both legs together.
>Thread the thinner end::Pass the thinner working end up through the bight.
>Go behind both legs::Carry the thin end around the back of the thicker rope's tail and standing part, in that order.
>Tuck beneath itself::Bring the thin end across the front and underneath its own earlier crossing, not back through the thick bight.
>Check the exits::Keep both tails on the same side, then pull the standing parts to snug the bend while leaving generous tails.

@shoelace-bow|Shoelace Bow|shoelace-bow-knot|Binding|1|Everyday|Standard shoelace knot
=Tie the familiar quick-release bow used on shoes.
+A shoe or two different-coloured practice cords.
!A bow that twists lengthwise may be based on a granny knot. Repeated loosening calls for checking the crossing directions and lace material.
?A balanced bow lies across the shoe rather than twisting along it.
xMaking both stages in the same handed direction forms a granny-style bow that often loosens.
>Make the first half knot::Cross the right lace over the left, tuck it underneath and draw the laces comfortably snug.
>Fold two loops::Make a bight in each lace, leaving enough tail beyond each loop.
>Reverse the crossing::Cross the left-side loop over the right-side loop, reversing the direction of the first half knot.
>Tuck one loop through::Feed that loop under and through the central opening, treating the folded loops as the two working ends.
>Balance the bow::Pull the loops outward and adjust all four sections. Check that the bow lies across the shoe and releases by pulling a tail.

@siberian-hitch|Siberian Hitch|siberian-hitch-knot|Quick release|2|Camping|Evenk hitch
=A slipped figure-eight attachment for a light camp ridgeline.
+Soft practice rope and a smooth post; keep hand wraps loose.
!A release tail can be snagged accidentally. Not for climbing or critical loads; never trap your hand in a tightening rope.
?A slipped figure-eight structure surrounds the standing part and slides up against the post.
xUsing only an overhand turn gives a different slipped hitch.
>Return across your hand::Lay the standing rope across an open hand, take the working end around the post, then bring it back across your hand.
>Go beneath the hand::Carry the working end below the hand while keeping the ropes loose.
>Circle both ropes::Bring it over both rope sections, then back underneath both, creating the figure-eight route.
>Pull a bight through::Fold the free tail and draw its bight through the loop held at your fingers. Remove your hand before tightening.
>Slide and check::Snug the slipped eight, then draw it against the post. With the line unloaded, pull the free tail to rehearse the release.

@slip-knot|Slip Knot|slip-knot|Quick release|1|Everyday,Decorative|Slipped overhand stopper
=A temporary stopper made by tucking a folded tail through an overhand loop.
+One soft practice rope.
!This is a quick-release stopper, not a reliable attachment. Its free tail can undo it with a pull.
?The bight comes from the short working end, rather than the long standing section.
xUsing a standing-part bight instead makes a different sliding-loop arrangement.
>Make a loose loop::Cross the short working end over the standing part.
>Fold the short end::Create a bight in that short end, leaving a free tail beyond the fold.
>Put the fold through::Pass the bight through the original loop instead of passing the whole end through.
>Snug around the bight::Draw the standing part to tighten the overhand structure around the folded section.
>Practice the release::Pull the free tail to withdraw the bight and undo the stopper. Keep the knot unloaded while learning.

@snell|Snell Knot|snell-knot|Fishing attachment|2|Fishing|Traditional snell
=Fasten a leader along a hook shank with a series of close wraps.
+Practice line and a blunt hook-shaped substitute.
!Protect sharp hook points. Hook-eye orientation matters; use the snelling method prescribed for the actual hook and rig.
?The coils surround the shank and line neatly, with the leader leaving in the intended direction through the eye.
xWrapping only the loose loop while missing the shank leaves the hook unsecured.
>Pass through the eye::Take the tag through the hook eye and lay it along the shank.
>Make a return loop::Bring a generous section back alongside the shank to create a loop below the eye.
>Wrap through the loop::Use the loop-side working section to wind several turns around the shank and the line lying against it.
>Hold the coils::Pinch the wrap stack so it cannot unwind while the remaining loop is drawn closed.
>Seat against the eye::Moisten actual line and pull the standing section to settle the coils toward the eye. Check the exit direction and test before trimming.

@square|Square Knot|square-knot|Binding|1|Everyday,Camping|Reef knot
=Close a light bundle with two opposite-handed half knots.
+Two ends of one cord around a soft practice bundle.
!A binding knot, not a dependable bend for joining loaded ropes. It can capsize or spill when the wrong ends are pulled.
?Both tails lie beside their own standing parts, and the finished knot is flat and symmetrical.
xRight-over-left twice makes a granny knot rather than a square knot.
>Cross right over left::Take the end on your right over the end on your left.
>Make the first half knot::Tuck it underneath and pull the ends gently apart around the bundle.
>Reverse the crossing::Now take the end on your left over the end on your right.
>Make the second half knot::Tuck it underneath to complete the opposite-handed second crossing.
>Flatten and inspect::Snug all four sections. The knot should lie flat, with each tail next to its own standing part; do not use it to join load-bearing ropes.

@stevedore|Stevedore Stopper|stevedore-stopper-knot|Stopper|2|Everyday,Boating|Stevedore knot
=A bulkier stopper with extra turns beyond a figure eight.
+One soft practice rope with a long working end.
!Check that the finished stopper is large enough for its opening. It may jam after a substantial load.
?The extra wraps remain neatly arranged around the standing part rather than overlapping randomly.
xOmitting the extra winding gives a smaller figure-eight stopper.
>Fold a bight::Make a U-shaped fold near the rope end, leaving a long working tail.
>Cross the standing part::Take the tail across the standing section to begin the winding.
>Make two full turns::Continue around the standing section for two complete turns, keeping the turns beside one another.
>Return through the bight::Pass the working tip down through the original folded opening after the two turns.
>Dress the stopper::Snug the winding section, then pull the standing part to grip the tail. Inspect the compact stopper and leave the tail visible.

@strangle|Strangle Knot|strangle-knot-using-end-method|Binding|2|Everyday,Boating|
=A tight double-overhand binding around a pole or bundle.
+Soft cord and a rounded practice object.
!Can grip so tightly it has to be cut. Never tie around a person, limb or animal.
?The tail passes under both wrapping turns, making a double-overhand structure around the object.
xAn incorrect tuck can leave only a half knot beside two loose turns.
>Make the first turn::Pass the working end around the object and bring it across the first turn.
>Wrap a second time::Continue around the object again, following the same general path beside the first wrap.
>Keep a passage open::Hold the crossing loose enough to see both turns and the space beneath them.
>Thread under both::Take the tail beneath both original turns in one pass, following the demonstrated rope path.
>Tighten evenly::Pull the two ends outward while arranging the coils side by side. Inspect before pulling it too tight to undo.

@surgeons-join|Surgeon's Join|surgeons-join-knot|Fishing join|1|Fishing|Double surgeon's knot
=Join two fishing lines with a shared double-overhand knot.
+Two practice lines with sufficient overlap; one section must pass through the loop.
!This is a fishing-line tutorial, not a medical procedure. Suitability depends on line materials and diameters.
?Both lines make two passes through the same shared opening, with all four ends accessible for setting.
xPassing just the tag tips while leaving a required line section outside the loop changes the join.
>Overlap the ends::Lay the leader and tippet side by side with their ends pointing in opposite directions.
>Make one shared loop::Treat both overlapping lines as a single doubled section and form an overhand opening.
>Pass the doubled section through::Take both relevant ends through the loop, drawing the full shorter section through when necessary.
>Repeat the pass::Take that same doubled section through the original opening a second time.
>Set all four parts::Moisten, keep both lines aligned and tighten by pulling all four sections. Inspect and test before trimming the tags.

@surgeons-loop|Surgeon's Loop|surgeons-loop-knot|Fishing loop|1|Fishing|Double overhand loop
=Create a fixed loop by tying a double overhand in doubled line.
+Practice monofilament or a fine soft cord.
!This fishing loop is not a medical or climbing technique. Avoid crossed strands while setting it.
?The doubled tip passes through the same opening twice, leaving a fixed eye beyond the knot.
xTwo separate knots in succession are not the same as two passes through one opening.
>Double the line::Fold enough line to form the desired eye plus working room for the knot.
>Make a doubled loop::Treat the folded section as a single strand and form an overhand crossing loop.
>Pass the bight once::Push the doubled tip through that opening, leaving everything slack.
>Pass the bight again::Take the same doubled tip through the same opening a second time.
>Set evenly::Moisten actual fishing line, align the paired strands and pull the eye and both line ends to snug the knot before inspecting and trimming.

@timber-hitch|Timber Hitch|timber-hitch-knot|Hitch|1|Camping,Boating|
=Wrap a rope around a pole with a tail that twists around its own returning section.
+Practice rope and a small wooden dowel.
!Needs continued tension and suitable friction. Real log handling or lifting requires additional controls and competent instruction.
?The tail twists around the returning rope, not around the standing part, and those twists are trapped against the object.
xTwisting around the wrong section or using too few turns can let the hitch slip.
>Go around the timber::Pass the working end around the practice pole or bundle.
>Circle the standing part::Take the tail around the long standing section once.
>Turn back on itself::Wrap the tail around its own returning section, heading back toward the timber.
>Add the turns::Make at least three turns for this small practice example, keeping them orderly. Actual material and diameter may require more.
>Seat against the object::Pull the standing part gently so the twisted section is gripped against the timber. Observe how the hitch loosens when tension is removed.

@trilene|Trilene Knot|trilene-knot|Fishing attachment|2|Fishing|
=Attach line to an eye with a double pass and a coiled finish.
+Practice monofilament and a blunt ring.
!The eye must accommodate two passes without damaging the line. Test the dressed knot with the actual line before fishing.
?Two strands run through the eye, and the tag passes through the paired opening beside it.
xThreading the tag through only one part of the eye-side double loop gives the wrong finish.
>Pass through twice::Feed the tag through the eye twice, leaving a small double loop beside the eye.
>Hold that opening::Pinch the double loop so it stays open while you make the wraps.
>Wind the standing line::Make five or six adjacent wraps around the standing line.
>Return through the double loop::Feed the tag through the original paired opening immediately beside the eye.
>Set and inspect::Moisten, pull the coils together evenly and seat them against the eye. Leave a visible tag until the knot has been checked and tested.

@truckers-hitch|Trucker's Hitch|truckers-hitch-knot|Tensioning system|3|Camping,Boating|Wagoner's hitch
=Use a rope loop as a simple pulley to tension a light practice line.
+Long practice rope and two smooth, firmly fixed supports; learn the Directional Figure-Eight Loop first.
!Mechanical advantage can overtighten or damage rope and objects. This is not vehicle cargo-securement instruction; use rated restraints and applicable rules for actual loads.
?The pulley loop faces the working direction, the tail returns through it from the far support, and two half hitches lock the tension.
xUsing an unsuitable or incorrectly oriented mid-rope loop can distort or release the system.
>Secure the first side::Attach one end to a practice support using an appropriate hitch and leave a long working section toward the other support.
>Make the pulley eye::Tie a Directional Figure-Eight Loop in the middle, oriented toward the working pull. Use that tutorial to check its full path.
>Go around the far support::Take the working end around the second support and bring it back toward the pulley eye.
>Return through the eye::Pass the tail through that eye and pull gently to take up slack. Keep fingers out of the moving loop.
>Lock the tension::Hold the adjustment while tying two same-direction half hitches below the pulley eye around the adjacent loaded section. Inspect before releasing your hand hold.

@tumble-hitch|Tumble Hitch|tumble-hitch-knot|Quick release|2|Camping|Tumblehitch
=A quick-release hitch built from interlocking bights around a pole.
+Soft practice rope and a horizontal post.
!The release tail must remain untouched. Never use for rappelling, personal support or any critical load.
?The second bight travels behind the pole and through the first; the final tail bight locks the second.
xConfusing it with the similar Highwayman's Hitch changes which bight carries the load.
>Hold the first bight::Fold a section of rope and hold its bight at the front of the pole.
>Pass the second behind::Take another bight behind the pole and push it through the first bight.
>Circle the standing part::Bring the remaining working tail around the standing section in the demonstrated direction.
>Add the release bight::Fold that tail and push its bight through the second bight to lock the arrangement.
>Snug and rehearse::Dress the nested bights with a light pull. Unload completely, then pull the release tail to dismantle the hitch.

@turks-head|Turk's Head|turks-head-knot|Decorative|3|Decorative|Woggle
=Weave a decorative band around a dowel or scarf-slide form.
+Long soft cord and a removable smooth cylinder.
!Decoration only. Keep any hand-held loops loose, and avoid heat-sealing near skin or while wearing the finished item.
?A continuous over-under braid circles the form, with each later circuit following the first alongside it.
xCrossing over a strand that should be an underpass interrupts the repeating braid.
>Make the starting wraps::Wrap the cord around the cylinder, crossing the second pass over the first to begin the braid.
>Weave the third pass::On the next circuit, take the working end alternately over and under the existing loops as demonstrated.
>Turn the work as you go::Rotate the cylinder to keep each new crossing in view. Preserve the alternating pattern all the way around.
>Follow the original path::When the end returns beside the starting strand, follow that strand around again, then once more, keeping the added passes parallel.
>Dress gradually::Move slack around the entire weave in small amounts. Tuck the ends inside and remove the cylinder only after the band holds its shape.

=Interlace three strands outward around a rope-end base.
+Three soft strands held together at their base.
!This is a decorative building block, not a full load-bearing rope termination.
?Every strand passes under its neighbour in a continuous cycle, with the final strand closing the first opening.

@water-knot|Water Knot|water-knot|Webbing join|2|Specialist,Camping|Tape knot,Ring bend
=Join flat webbing by retracing an overhand knot in reverse.
+Two contrasting lengths of flat practice webbing.
!Can loosen through repeated cycling or snagging. Critical webbing systems require qualified instruction, adequate tails and repeated inspection.
?The second strip follows the first strip's complete overhand path, lying flat with no folds or twists.

@windsor|Windsor Necktie Knot|tie-a-windsor-necktie-knot|Necktie|2|Neckties|Full Windsor,Double Windsor
=A broad, symmetrical triangular necktie knot.
+A sufficiently long fabric tie and a mirror.
!Uses more tie length than a Four-in-Hand. Keep the finished collar comfortable rather than pulling it tight against the neck.
?A shoulder turn on each side supports a smooth horizontal front band.

@zeppelin-bend|Zeppelin Bend|zeppelin-bend-knot|Bend|3|Camping,Boating|Rosendahl bend
