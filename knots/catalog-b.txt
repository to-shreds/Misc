@davy|Davy Knot|davy-knot|Fishing attachment|1|Fishing|
=A tiny knot for attaching a fly or small hook to a tippet.
+Fine practice line and a blunt practice ring.
!Not every line size or material suits this compact knot. Test before use and keep sharp hooks covered while learning.
?The tag is trapped against the eye beneath the knot, not displaced toward the middle of the eye.
xPulling only the standing line before dressing can distort the tiny crossing.
>Thread the eye::Pass the tag through the eye and bring it beside the long line.
>Make the half hitch::Take the tag around the long line and back through the eye-side loop. Keep that small half hitch open.
>Continue around the loop::Carry the tag around and back through the same loop a second time, as shown. Do not substitute a loose figure eight.
>Position the tag::Keep the tag close against the eye, beneath the crossing that will trap it.
>Set with your fingers::Draw the knot to the eye carefully, using your fingers rather than your teeth to hold the tag. Inspect and test before trimming.

@distel-hitch|Distel Hitch|distel-hitch-knot|Friction hitch|3|Specialist|
=A one-direction gripping hitch used in arborist rope systems.
+Two suitable soft practice ropes and no suspended load.
!Not a climbing instruction. Correct cord, rope diameter, end terminations and system configuration require qualified supervision.
?A stack of four adjacent upper turns and one lower turn forms the gripping structure; both attachment legs leave below it.
xReversing the direction of the intended pull can prevent the hitch gripping as expected.
>Position the cord::Lay the working cord alongside the host rope, leaving enough at the lower end for its prescribed attachment.
>Build the upper wraps::Wrap the working end around the host rope four times, progressing upward with even, adjacent coils.
>Return downward::Bring the working end down along the outside of the coils.
>Complete the lower turn::Take one turn below the main stack and tuck to create the clove-hitch-style finishing arrangement shown in the demonstration.
>Dress without loading::Bring both cord legs together and snug the wraps. Study the prescribed end terminations separately with a qualified instructor.

@double-davy|Double Davy Knot|double-davy-knot|Fishing attachment|2|Fishing|
=Add a second locking tuck to the compact Davy fishing knot.
+Practice fishing line and a blunt ring.
!Line material matters. The extra tuck does not make a poorly dressed knot reliable.
?Three passes complete the loop, with the tag captured against the eye underneath the finished knot.
xMistaking an ordinary double overhand knot for the Double Davy misses its eye-side crossing.
>Thread and half hitch::Pass the tag through the eye, around the long line and back through the first loop to form a loose half hitch.
>Make the second pass::Continue around and back through the loop, beginning the ordinary Davy pattern.
>Add the extra pass::Take the tag around the long line once more and through the loop a third time.
>Keep the tag beside the eye::Arrange the final tuck so the tag will be enclosed beneath the knot against the eye.
>Dress and test::Use your fingers to control the tag while drawing the knot snug. Inspect its eye-side position and test before trimming.

@spanish-bowline|Spanish Bowline|spanish-bowline-knot|Fixed loop|3|Boating,Specialist|Spanish double loop
=Form two adjacent eyes from a carefully arranged central loop.
+Long soft practice rope on a flat table.
!Do not treat this as a harness, rescue seat or automatically balanced anchor. The loops need careful dressing.
?Each side of the central loop has been drawn through its corresponding side loop.
xPulling only one side through the centre does not complete the pair of eyes.
>Make the large loop::Lay a broad loop in the rope, keeping both standing sections visible.
>Tuck it beneath the standing sections::Move the loop under the two sections leading away from it, preserving the original crossing.
>Fold both sides inward::Fold one half across the centre, then fold the opposite half across. You should now see two side openings and one central opening.
>Draw out the eyes::Take one side of the central loop up through its neighbouring side opening; repeat on the other side.
>Balance and dress::Pull the two new eyes evenly, work out trapped slack and compare the crossing pattern before snugging the knot.

@double-fishermans|Double Fisherman's Bend|double-fishermans-bend-knot|Bend|2|Camping,Specialist|Grapevine bend
=Join similar cords with two opposing double-overhand barrels.
+Two similar practice cords, preferably different colours.
!Can jam very tightly. Rope-system use requires specified materials and tail lengths; some cords require a different termination.
?Each tail makes two turns around the other standing part. The two barrels meet squarely.
xWrapping only one turn produces the single fisherman's version, not this knot.
>Overlap the ropes::Place the two ends alongside one another, pointing in opposite directions, with generous overlap.
>Make the first barrel::Wrap one tail twice around the other rope's standing part and its own returning section.
>Tuck through both turns::Feed that tail back through the centre of both wraps. Snug into a neat barrel.
>Repeat at the other end::Use the other tail to make the same double-overhand barrel around the first rope's standing part.
>Bring the barrels together::Pull the two standing parts apart. Inspect both sides and both tails before the barrels tighten against each other.

@double-overhand|Double Overhand Stopper|double-overhand-stopper-knot|Stopper|1|Everyday,Camping,Boating|Double overhand knot
=A compact stopper with an extra turn inside an overhand knot.
+One soft rope.
!Can jam after heavy loading. It is a stopper, not a loop or a rope join.
?Two close turns form a small barrel around the exiting rope section.
xMaking two separate overhand knots does not create a double overhand stopper.
>Make a crossing loop::Cross the working end over the standing part, leaving a long tail.
>Pass through once::Feed the tail through the loop as you would for an ordinary overhand knot.
>Pass through again::Before tightening, feed the same tail around and through that same opening a second time.
>Arrange the coils::Hold the two wraps adjacent, rather than allowing one to climb over the other.
>Snug the stopper::Pull the tail and standing part apart gradually until a compact barrel forms.

@dropper-loop|Dropper Loop|dropper-loop-knot|Fishing loop|2|Fishing|
=Form a side loop in the middle of a fishing line.
+Practice line with room for a broad central loop.
!A side loop is not the same as a strong main-line termination. Match the rig and line material to the intended fishing use.
?Even twists sit on both sides of a central loop that exits between them.
xPulling a tag or standing end through instead of the large loop gives the wrong knot.
>Make a broad loop::Cross a middle section of line over itself, without using either end.
>Twist around the crossing::Wrap one side of the loop around the adjacent line several times, keeping a small central gap open.
>Preserve the gap::Pinch the centre so the twists divide to either side and the opening remains accessible.
>Pull the loop through::Push the opposite side of the large loop through that central opening, making the dropper eye.
>Set evenly::Hold the new eye while drawing the two standing sections apart. Moisten actual fishing line and ease both twist stacks together.

@egg-loop|Egg Loop Knot|egg-loop-knot|Fishing attachment|3|Fishing|Bumper knot
=Tie a hook with a movable loop that can hold soft bait.
+Practice leader and a blunt hook substitute.
!Keep actual hook points covered during practice. Bait-loop size and wrap count depend on the line and hook.
?The coils lie around the shank and leader; pushing line back through the eye opens the bait loop.
xLosing tension on the first coil stack while feeding the long end leaves loose wraps.
>Lay a short section along the hook::Pass one leader end through the eye and hold the short section against the shank.
>Make the first wrap stack::Use the long section to wrap around the shank and short section about fifteen times for this demonstrated method.
>Return the long end through the eye::Keep the wraps pinched while passing the long end through the eye again, forming a large loop beside the hook.
>Wrap with the new loop::Use that loop to make about seven more wraps around the shank and line, keeping all coils controlled and uncrossed.
>Draw closed and inspect::Pull the long end to close the loop and set the wraps. Push a little line back through the eye to open and check the bait loop.

@farrimond|Farrimond Friction Hitch|farrimond-friction-hitch-knot|Friction hitch|2|Camping|Quick-release guyline hitch
=An adjustable, quick-release hitch for a light tent ridgeline.
+Soft practice cord and a fixed post.
!A pull on the release tail undoes it. Performance depends on rope friction; never use for personal support.
?A doubled loop makes the gripping wraps, and the final tuck is a release bight rather than the bare tip.
xWrapping the bare tail instead of the doubled loop creates another hitch.
>Return from the post::Take the working end around the support and bring it back beside the standing part.
>Make a small loop::Form a loop in the returning section, leaving enough free end for a finishing bight.
>Wrap the loop twice::Carry this loop twice around the standing part, much like wrapping a Prusik loop. Keep both strands of each wrap neat.
>Insert the release bight::Fold the remaining working end and push its bight through the end of the wrapped loop.
>Snug and adjust::Tighten the wraps around the standing part. Slide only when unloaded, and keep the release tail out of the way.

@bottle-sling|Bottle Sling|bottle-sling-knot|Hitch|3|Everyday,Decorative|Jug sling
=Arrange an adjustable collar and carrying loops around a bottle neck.
+Soft cord and an empty plastic bottle with a pronounced neck lip.
!Practice near a tabletop with an empty plastic bottle. Not for glass, heavy containers or suspending anything above a person.
?The central collar seats beneath the bottle's lip, while the larger loops form the handles.
xUsing a straight-sided container without a retaining lip can let the sling slide off.
>Lay out a broad loop::Arrange a large rope loop on the table and make a small inward bight along one side.
>Fold in the opposite side::Bring a second bight downward from the opposite side of the large loop.
>Interlock the bights::Tuck that second bight under the first, creating a new small opening.
>Bring the handles through::Pass the far end of the large loop beneath the new opening, then pass the near end beneath the original bight, following the demonstration.
>Fit and inspect::Open the centre over the empty bottle neck, snug it beneath the lip and gently bring the handle loops together while keeping the bottle supported.

@figure-eight-bend|Figure-Eight Bend|figure-8-bend-knot|Bend|2|Camping,Specialist|Flemish bend,Figure 8 bend
=Join two ropes by retracing a figure-eight knot in reverse.
+Two similar ropes in different colours.
!Not the offset figure-eight bend. Rope-joining for climbing or rescue requires qualified instruction and equipment-specific procedures.
?Every part of the original eight has a parallel companion, with the ropes leaving in opposite directions.
xDo not tie both ends together as a single offset figure eight; that is a different knot.
>Make a loose eight::Tie a figure-eight stopper in the end of the first rope, leaving all its openings loose.
>Enter from the tail::Bring the second rope's tail alongside the first rope's tail and start following it backward into the knot.
>Retrace the full path::Take the second tail beside the first strand around the central crossing and through both lobes of the eight.
>Keep the pair parallel::Work out any crossed or stacked sections so the two ropes follow one another consistently.
>Set all four parts::Pull each tail and each standing part in turn, then draw the standing parts apart while checking generous tails remain.

@figure-eight-directional|Directional Figure-Eight Loop|figure-8-directional-loop-knot|Fixed loop|3|Specialist|Inline figure eight,Directional figure 8
=A mid-rope loop arranged for pull in one particular direction.
+Long soft practice rope with the intended pull direction marked.
!Directional: pulling against the wrong leg may distort the knot. Do not use for personal support from this description.
?The eye runs alongside the intended loaded standing section, not across the knot in the opposite direction.
xTreating it as a multidirectional butterfly loop ignores its directional behaviour.
>Identify the standing side::Lay the rope straight and mark which section is intended to receive the pull along the rope.
>Make a crossing loop::Form a loop in the middle of the rope, keeping its crossing oriented as in the demonstration.
>Carry the loop behind::Take the whole loop behind the marked standing section, preserving the original crossing.
>Continue around and through::Bring the loop around that section and through the opening beside the other rope section, making the directional figure-eight path.
>Dress and check the direction::Align the eye with the intended loaded section and snug the strands. Use only a light hand test in that direction, not a suspended load.

@figure-eight-double-loop|Double Figure-Eight Loop|figure-8-double-loop-knot|Fixed loop|3|Specialist|Bunny ears,Figure 8 double loop
=Create a pair of adjacent fixed eyes from a doubled rope section.
+About two metres of soft practice rope.
!Two eyes are not automatically equalized anchors or a harness. Technical rigging requires instruction beyond this guide.
?The finishing collar surrounds both loop roots and all doubled strands are neatly arranged.
xMissing the fold-over collar leaves the two eyes unsecured.
>Double a long section::Make a generous bight, then arrange the doubled rope into the start of a figure eight.
>Make the eight's turn::Carry the doubled tip around the standing pair and back toward the original opening.
>Push a fold, not the whole tip::Feed a folded section through the opening, leaving the original tip as a separate loop outside the knot.
>Fold over the collar::Spread that original tip loop and take it over both newly formed eyes, continuing behind them to the base of the knot.
>Balance the eyes::Adjust the two eye sizes while everything is loose, then snug every paired strand and compare the complete structure with the demonstration.

@figure-eight-loop|Figure-Eight Follow-Through Loop|figure-8-follow-through-loop-knot|Fixed loop|2|Camping,Specialist|Rewoven figure eight,Figure 8 follow through
=Form a fixed eye through a closed ring by retracing an eight.
+Soft practice rope and a closed practice ring, not a worn harness.
!This page is not a climbing tie-in lesson. Use qualified instruction and the harness and rope manufacturers' complete procedure for actual climbing.
?Two parallel strands trace the entire eight; the tail emerges beside the standing part with no hidden crossover.
xSkipping one of the eight's lobes creates an incomplete rethread.
>Tie the starting eight::Make a loose figure-eight stopper well back from the rope end, leaving a long tail.
>Go around the attachment::Take the tail through the practice ring or around the object to create the desired eye.
>Enter beside the tail::Start retracing the original eight backward, placing the working end beside the strand leading into the eye.
>Follow every bend::Continue alongside the original rope through each turn until the tail emerges beside the standing part.
>Dress and inspect::Arrange paired strands without crossing them. Pull all four sections progressively and check the entire rope path before setting.

@figure-eight|Figure-Eight Stopper|figure-8-knot|Stopper|1|Everyday,Camping,Boating|Figure 8 knot,Flemish knot
=A simple, easily recognized stopper at a rope end.
+One soft rope with a visible working end.
!Can work loose when repeatedly shaken. Check suitability for the opening and application; not a rope join.
?The loose knot clearly traces an eight, with two lobes and one central crossing.
xGoing directly through the first loop makes an overhand knot instead.
>Cross the rope::Place the working end over the standing part to make a small loop.
>Go behind::Carry the working end behind the standing part, continuing around it rather than immediately through the loop.
>Come back to the loop::Bring the working end forward and line it up with the original loop.
>Thread through::Pass the tip through the original loop. The loose path should now look like a figure eight.
>Set gently::Pull the tail and standing part apart while preserving the two-lobed shape and a visible tail.

@figure-nine-loop|Figure-Nine Loop|figure-9-loop-knot|Fixed loop|2|Specialist,Camping|Figure 9 loop
=A fixed loop made with a bight and an extra half turn beyond a figure eight.
+One long soft practice rope.
!More turns do not make every rope system safer. Technical use requires the specified knot and material, not visual similarity.
?The doubled rope follows a nine-like path with its extra turn intact and strands kept parallel.
xOmitting the extra half turn produces a figure-eight loop instead.
>Double the rope::Make a long bight and treat both of its strands as one working section.
>Make the starting loop::Cross the doubled bight over the doubled standing section.
>Add the extra turn::Carry the bight around the standing pair, continuing an extra half turn beyond the usual figure-eight route.
>Feed through the opening::Bring the bight through the starting opening without twisting the doubled strands.
>Dress the turns::Pull the eye and standing pair while easing each parallel strand into place. Leave the knot loose enough to study before setting.

@flat-overhand-bend|Flat Overhand Bend|flat-overhand-bend-knot|Bend|2|Specialist|Offset overhand bend
=An offset rope join whose shape differs from a rethreaded overhand.
+Two similar practice ropes; use contrasting colours.
!Critical-use suitability, long tails and setting requirements demand qualified instruction. Do not use short illustrative tails or substitute an offset figure eight.
?Both standing parts emerge together on one side; both long tails emerge together on the other.
xConfusing a longer-looking offset figure eight with a safer version is a dangerous substitution.
>Align the ends::Lay the two tails together, pointing in the same direction, leaving generous working length.
>Make one loop::Treat the paired tails as a single strand and form an overhand crossing loop.
>Pass both tails through::Feed both tails through the same opening together, without adding an extra wrap.
>Dress the pair::Keep the ropes side by side and eliminate all loose pockets inside the knot.
>Set each strand::Pull each standing part and each tail individually, then inspect the finished offset structure. Practice only, without a suspended load.

@four-in-hand|Four-in-Hand Necktie|tie-a-four-in-hand-necktie-knot|Necktie|1|Neckties|Schoolboy knot
=A slim, slightly asymmetrical necktie knot.
+A fabric necktie and a mirror; start loose.
!Use fabric neckwear only and keep comfortable space at the collar. Never tighten practice cord around the neck.
?The wide blade covers the narrow end and passes through a single front band.
xStarting with too little wide-end length leaves the finished tie too short.
>Set the lengths::Drape the tie face outward, wide end longer. Cross the wide end over the narrow end.
>Go behind::Bring the wide end behind the narrow end and out to the original side.
>Make the front band::Sweep the wide end across the front again, keeping a finger beneath this new band.
>Come up through the collar::Bring the wide end upward through the neck opening from behind.
>Go down through the band::Feed it down through the front band, then hold the narrow end while gently sliding and shaping the knot.

@gnat-hitch|Gnat Hitch|gnat-hitch-knot|Hitch|2|Everyday,Camping|
=A compact hitch with a simple extra tuck through a half hitch.
+Practice cord and a smooth dowel.
!Do not extrapolate a small practice hitch to a safety-critical attachment. Check the material and load direction.
?The tail circles the standing part again and returns through the original half-hitch opening.
xAdding a second independent half hitch makes a different structure.
>Go around the post::Take the working end around the dowel and bring it beside the standing part.
>Make a loose half hitch::Wrap the tail around the standing part and tuck through its own loop. Keep the loop open.
>Circle behind again::Take the tail behind the standing part a second time.
>Use the original opening::Pass it down through the loop of the existing half hitch, not through a new separate loop.
>Draw to the object::Pull the standing part while guiding the hitch against the dowel, then snug the tail and inspect the crossing.

@half-hitch|Half Hitch|half-hitch-knot|Hitch|1|Everyday,Camping|
=The basic finishing turn used inside many other hitches.
+Practice cord and a small post.
!One half hitch alone is usually not a secure attachment. Treat it as a building block or follow a specified complete system.
?The tail circles the standing part and is caught under its own turn.
xA simple wrap around the post with no tuck is not a half hitch.
>Pass around the object::Take the working end around a post and bring it back beside the standing part.
>Cross the standing part::Carry the tail over the standing part to make a small opening beside it.
>Go around and through::Continue the tail behind the standing part and through the opening made by the crossing.
>Snug lightly::Pull the tail just enough to make the crossing clear without jamming it.
>Learn the limitation::Notice how readily a single half hitch can shift. Continue to Two Half Hitches for a fuller attachment pattern.

@half-windsor|Half Windsor|tie-a-half-windsor-necktie-knot|Necktie|2|Neckties|Half-Windsor knot
=A medium-sized, symmetrical necktie knot.
+A fabric necktie and mirror.
!Keep neckwear comfortably loose. Practice with fabric rather than cord.
?A single diagonal shoulder sits behind a smooth front band, creating a moderately triangular knot.
xPassing the wide end over the front when it should go behind reverses the forming shoulder.
>Cross and go behind::With the wide end longer and the tie face outward, cross wide over narrow, then carry wide behind narrow.
>Build one shoulder::Bring the wide end up and down through the neck opening, forming a turn on that side.
>Make the front band::Sweep the wide end horizontally across the front of the narrow end and forming knot.
>Come up from behind::Bring the wide end upward through the neck opening, keeping the front band loose.
>Thread and shape::Feed the wide blade down through the front band. Gently snug, centre and shape the knot.

@crown-sinnet|Crown Sinnet|crown-sinnet-knot|Decorative|2|Decorative|Box braid,Scoubidou,Boondoggle
=Build a square decorative braid from alternating four-strand crowns.
+Four soft coloured strands secured together at one end.
!Decoration only. Do not use as a safety tether or make non-breakaway neckwear.
?Each layer contains four interlocking folds; alternating direction keeps the braid square instead of spiralling.
xAlways crowning in the same direction creates a spiral rather than this box-shaped braid.
>Arrange the four ends::Secure their bases together and spread the working tips toward four sides.
>Start a crown::Fold the first strand over its neighbour, then fold that neighbour over the next. Keep the first fold open.
>Close the fourth fold::Continue around all four strands and pass the last strand through the opening left by the first. Snug all four evenly.
>Reverse the next layer::Make another four-strand crown in the opposite circular direction.
>Continue and finish::Alternate directions, tightening each layer before starting the next. Finish with an appropriate craft binding, such as whipping the ends together.

@halyard-hitch|Halyard Hitch|halyard-hitch-knot|Hitch|2|Boating|
=A compact rope-to-shackle attachment with two wrapping turns.
+Practice rope and a small smooth ring or shackle.
!Not suitable around a large-diameter object. Can jam; do not copy very short tails from illustrations for critical loads.
?Two turns surround the standing part, and the tail exits through the small opening next to the shackle.
xTaking the tail through the wrong side of the coil stack prevents the intended compact finish.
>Thread the shackle::Feed a generous tail through the shackle and return it beside the standing rope.
>Make the first wrap::Take the tail once around the standing part.
>Add the second wrap::Continue around the standing part again, keeping the wraps adjacent.
>Tuck beside the shackle::Pass the tail through the small loop remaining between the shackle and the wraps.
>Dress without trimming::Draw the knot snugly against the shackle, pulling out slack progressively. Leave a visible practice tail rather than cutting or heating it.

@heaving-line|Heaving Line Knot|heaving-line-knot|Stopper|2|Boating,Decorative|
=Make a soft coil bundle at the end of a light line.
+Soft rope only, without any hard or weighted insert.
!Never add metal, stones or other hard weights. Do not throw toward people, animals, vehicles or fragile objects.
?The early turns surround two strands; the later turns surround three, with the tail retained through the end loop.
xPulling the bight closed before placing the tail through it leaves the coil bundle unfinished.
>Fold the rope::Make a long bight near the end and position it so the working tail lies inside the folded arrangement.
>Start around two strands::Use the working tail to make the initial wraps around the two appropriate strands shown in the demonstration.
>Enclose the third strand::Continue winding around all three parallel strands, adding close coils as you use up the working tail.
>Pass through the end loop::Feed the remaining tail through the loop left at the end of the coil bundle.
>Dress the soft bundle::Work the coils together and close the retaining loop around the tail. Keep the knot entirely soft and unweighted.
